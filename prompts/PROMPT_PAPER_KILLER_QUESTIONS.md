Yes. The system is strong, but it needs one load-bearing correction:

**99 and 94 are not two names for the same opening. They run on different psychological engines.**

* **99 — Impossible Statement:** resolve the surface impossibility quickly, then reveal the deeper impossibility created by that resolution.
* **94 — Paradox:** keep the original tension alive across the paper and resolve it near the end.

That distinction should be written directly into the system so nobody later runs 99 like a slower version of 94.

## SECTION 1 — THE SEVEN BASE DEVICES

99 — Impossible Statement: Present a claim the reader initially rejects. Resolve the obvious impossibility quickly, then show that the resolution exposes a deeper impossibility.

98 — Dangerous Question: Ask a question whose honest answer threatens an existing belief, framework, or identity.

97 — Human Moment: Begin with a recognizable experience, wound, choice, loss, or longing that gives the abstract argument emotional reality.

96 — Broken Assumption: Identify an assumption the reader has treated as natural, then show that the argument changes when that assumption is removed.

95 — Story Hook: Open with an event whose meaning is incomplete. Return to it as the paper changes what the event means.

94 — Paradox: Present two truths that appear unable to coexist. Keep the tension alive until the paper earns their reconciliation.

93 — Cost: State what is at stake if the paper is right, wrong, ignored, or misunderstood.

## SECTION 2 — THE TWO LONG-FORM TENSION ENGINES

### 99 — RESOLVE AND REPLACE

The Impossible Statement should not remain obviously false for too long.

The reader must receive an early payoff.

At the A-stage, resolve the surface impossibility.

But the resolution must expose a deeper problem that the paper will carry forward.

Structure:

Surface impossibility

→ Fast clarification

→ Surface impossibility resolved

→ Hidden assumption exposed

→ Deeper impossibility opened

The deeper impossibility must grow naturally out of the first one. It cannot feel like a topic switch.

Test:

Can the surface impossibility be resolved without invoking the deeper problem?

If yes, the handoff is probably artificial.

If no, the deeper impossibility was present inside the opening all along.

Example:

99 — “Two plus two can equal five.”

99A — “Not in ordinary arithmetic. But saying that already assumes that ‘two,’ ‘plus,’ and ‘equals’ possess stable meaning.”

The cheap impossibility is closed.

The expensive impossibility has opened:

What makes mathematical meaning stable enough for two plus two to be unable to equal five?

---

### 94 — SUSTAIN AND RESOLVE

The Paradox is not paid off immediately.

Its power comes from remaining unresolved while evidence accumulates.

Structure:

Two accepted truths

→ Apparent contradiction

→ Competing interpretations

→ Formal pressure

→ Deeper reformulation

→ Final reconciliation

The paradox should return throughout the paper with increasing precision.

The opening version may be intuitive.

The middle version becomes conceptual.

The formal version becomes mathematical or structural.

The final version becomes resolvable.

Example:

94 — “Reality appears mathematical. Meaning appears personal. Why should both be true?”

94A — Clarify why neither side can simply be discarded.

94B — Reveal the assumption that makes them look incompatible.

94C — Connect the paradox to the formal model.

94D — Show what changes if both are projections of one deeper structure.

94E — Reconcile them without reducing either one to the other.

The paradox does not disappear.

The reader becomes capable of seeing why it was never a contradiction in the first place.

## SECTION 3 — THE STAGE LETTERS

Base number — Introduction: the device appears for the first time and creates the paper’s central promise.

A — Early development: the reader receives enough clarification, orientation, or partial resolution to continue.

B — Hidden substrate: reveal the assumption, structure, or deeper question beneath the opening.

C — Midpoint return: connect the originating device to the formal argument, evidence, or central derivation.

C2 — Second midpoint return: test the device against the argument’s weakest link or strongest objection.

D — Late return: show the larger consequence, human meaning, or transformed interpretation.

E — Final resolution: return to the opening in its original key and reveal what it means after the argument.

E′ — Productive reversal: reveal that the opening asked the wrong question, used the wrong frame, or misunderstood the true problem.

## SECTION 4 — STAGE-SPECIFIC RULES

### A — EARLY PAYMENT

The A-stage must reduce resistance without destroying curiosity.

The reader should leave A with a better question, not with no question.

For 99, A resolves the surface impossibility and opens the deeper one.

For 94, A sharpens the paradox without resolving it.

For 95, A explains why the original interpretation of the story was incomplete.

For 93, A makes the cost concrete rather than merely dramatic.

Rule:

Pay enough trust to keep the reader.

Do not pay the entire debt.

---

### B — THE SUBSTRATE

The B-stage reveals what the opening was actually about.

This is usually where the reader discovers that the surface topic was only the visible layer.

Examples:

The arithmetic claim was really about intelligibility.

The personal story was really about the wrong frame.

The paradox was really about a hidden category distinction.

The cost was really about what the reader’s present model cannot explain.

B is where the paper descends beneath the event into the governing structure.

---

### C — FORMAL CONTACT

The C-stage prevents the motif from remaining decorative.

The opening device must now touch the formal argument.

The equation, model, evidence, or derivation should illuminate the opening.

The opening should also help the reader understand why the formal material matters.

If the formal section could be removed without affecting the motif, or the motif could be removed without affecting the formal section, the connection is ornamental rather than load-bearing.

---

### C2 — ADVERSARIAL RETURN

Use C2 only in longer papers.

C2 is not another reminder.

It is the moment where the motif meets the strongest objection.

Examples:

The impossible statement faces the possibility that its deeper problem is only semantic.

The paradox faces the claim that one side should simply be reduced to the other.

The story hook faces the possibility that the personal event proves nothing general.

The cost opening faces the objection that the stakes have been exaggerated.

C2 exists to keep the motif alive under pressure.

---

### D — CONSEQUENCE

The D-stage shows what the argument changes.

This may be:

Scientific consequence

Theological consequence

Personal consequence

Methodological consequence

Civilizational consequence

D should expand the meaning of the opening without resolving it prematurely.

---

### E — TRANSFORMED RETURN

The E-stage returns to the opening with accumulated meaning.

The wording may repeat.

The meaning must not.

A strong E-stage makes the reader hear the opening again from a higher level.

Rule:

The paper should usually resolve in the key it opened in.

A Cost paper should return to cost.

A Story paper should return to the story.

A Paradox paper should reconcile the paradox.

An Impossible Statement paper should reveal why the original impossibility was evidence of the deeper order.

---

### E′ — PRODUCTIVE REVERSAL

Sometimes the strongest ending does not answer the opening.

It reveals that the opening was built on the wrong question.

Example:

Opening:

“Why does evil always seem stronger than good?”

Ending:

“The question was never why evil appears stronger. The question was why evil requires the good it tries to destroy.”

E′ does not abandon the opening.

It exposes its hidden error.

Use E′ only when the entire paper has earned the reversal.

---

## SECTION 5 — TRANSITION CODES

T1 — Clarification: define what the opening does and does not mean.

T2 — Hidden Assumption: expose the premise beneath the reader’s first interpretation.

T3 — Personal Cost: move from abstract claim to human consequence.

T4 — Evidence or Observation: ground the opening in something visible, measurable, historical, or widely recognizable.

T5 — Deepening Question: replace the first question with the more difficult question hidden inside it.

T6 — Reversal: show that the reader’s first interpretation points in the wrong direction.

T7 — Promise of Resolution: tell the reader what kind of journey will be required to settle the opening.

Transitions are optional.

Use a T-code only when the movement between stages would otherwise feel abrupt or deceptive.

A psychologically necessary next question can perform the transition without a separate bridge paragraph.

When a motif disappears for too long, its unresolved tension weakens.

## The numbering system

The **base number** identifies the original opening strategy.

```text
99 — Impossible Statement
98 — Dangerous Question
97 — Human Moment
96 — Broken Assumption
95 — Story Hook
94 — Paradox
93 — Cost
```

The base number by itself means:

> This is how the device appears in the introduction.

Then letters mark its later appearances:

```text
99     = Impossible Statement in the introduction
99A    = Its development in paragraph two
99B    = Its next development
99C    = Its midpoint return
99D    = Its late-paper return
99E    = Its final resolution
```

So the number preserves the **idea**, while the letter preserves the **stage of the paper**.

# Example: 99 — The Impossible Statement

### 99 — Introduction

> Two plus two can equal five.

The reader immediately rejects it.

### 99A — Paragraph Two

Clarify that the claim does not concern ordinary arithmetic:

> In arithmetic, two plus two does not equal five. But arithmetic already assumes that “two,” “plus,” and “equals” possess stable meaning.

Now we have given the reader enough resolution to continue, while opening the deeper question.

### 99B — Paragraph Three

Reveal the hidden substrate:

> The paradox is not mathematical. It concerns what must exist before mathematics can be intelligible.

### 99C — Midpoint Return

Connect the original statement to the formal argument:

> Two plus two cannot equal five precisely because intelligibility is constrained by an order deeper than individual symbols.

### 99D — Late Return

Show the larger consequence:

> Mathematics does not create consistency. It expresses a consistency it already depends upon.

### 99E — Final Resolution

Return to the opening with transformed meaning:

> Two plus two never equaled five. The impossibility was the evidence: truth was governing the equation before we ever calculated it.

That is cumulative recurrence. Every return adds something.

PART 1: COGNITIVE PERSUASION TECHNIQUES
1. Primacy and Recency Effects

People remember the first and last things they encounter. Everything in the middle fades.

Application:

Present your strongest argument FIRST — it anchors everything that follows
Restate your core conclusion LAST — it's the final imprint
The middle carries supporting evidence, but the bookends carry conviction
Use Roman numerals or visual weight (headers, bold) on your primary argument
Leave competing arguments unmarked or visually diminished — less formatting = less cognitive weight
2. Anchoring Bias

The first number/idea/frame presented becomes the reference point for everything after.

Application:

Open with YOUR frame, not the opposition's
If presenting multiple options (e.g., worldviews), present yours first and strongest
The reader judges all subsequent options relative to the first one they absorbed
Never lead with the counterargument — you'll anchor the reader in it
3. Cognitive Fluency

People prefer information that is easy to process. Fluent = trustworthy in the brain.

Application:

Short sentences for key claims
Familiar metaphors (operating systems, gravity, signal/noise)
Clean formatting — white space, headers, bullet points
Rhythmic prose — read aloud to check flow
If it's hard to read, the brain flags it as suspicious. If it's smooth, the brain flags it as true.
4. Contrast and Scarcity Bias

What is emphasized or set apart seems more important. What is rare seems more valuable.

Application:

When you have one truth among many options, make it visually distinct
Use formatting (bold, isolation, spacing) to elevate key claims
Make the reader feel they are discovering something rare and precious
Frame your framework as the one coherent option among many incoherent ones
5. The Socratic Backflip (The Judo Move)

Ask the reader a question whose honest answer leads them directly to your conclusion — without you having to state it.

Application:

"What do you want in a God?" → Any honest answer describes Jesus
"What would a maximally coherent system look like?" → The reader derives the Logos
The reader convinces themselves. You just held the mirror.
This is the most powerful persuasion technique in the entire toolkit.
6. Binary Framing at the Decision Point

When it's time for the reader to choose, reduce options to two. No middle ground.

Application:

"Either you want the God who defeats death and defines goodness, or you want a god that lets you keep pretending."
Binary framing drives clarity and eliminates the comfortable middle
Use it sparingly — only at the climax, never throughout
7. Identity Projection

Instead of attacking the reader's position, invite them to see who they are by what they want.

Application:

"Your answer doesn't just define Him — it defines you"
"If you want mercy, maybe you know you need it"
This bypasses defensiveness because you're not accusing — you're mirroring
8. Cognitive Dissonance Pressure

Present the reader with a contradiction they can't resolve without changing their mind.

Application:

Show that their stated values (wanting truth, justice, love) logically require your conclusion
Don't resolve the tension for them immediately — let it sit
Use strategic bold/italics at the dissonance point (e.g., "not fundamental" bold, "emergent" italic)
The discomfort drives the insight
9. The Resummarization Technique

After presenting your conclusion, resummarize it one more time. Captures both primacy and recency simultaneously.

Application:

End each section with a one-line restatement of the core claim
End the paper with a compressed version of the entire argument
The reader walks away carrying your signal, not the noise
PART 2: READER MANAGEMENT TECHNIQUES
10. Mere Exposure Effect

Familiar terms become more persuasive. We prefer what we recognize.

Application:

Format key terms consistently and visually distinctly (e.g., "Logos Field (χ)")
Ensure the core term appears at least once per section heading
Don't let your central concept disappear into paragraph text
Repetition of key terms increases perceived truthfulness
11. Credibility Anchors Before Radical Claims

Before making bold ontological claims, briefly invoke an authority figure.
or oh no if we do one of the 7 which 
Application:

"As Wheeler foresaw…" before "The Logos is not a metaphor, it is a physical necessity"
This softens reader resistance to paradigm shifts
You need credibility scaffolding before flipping their worldview
The authority doesn't prove your point — it gives the reader permission to consider it
12. Reader Agreement Checkpoints

Insert occasional rhetorical nods that validate the reader's intelligence.

Application:

"You've likely intuited this yourself…"
"You may already sense where this is going…"
Creates cognitive consonance — the reader feels smart
Increases trust and engagement because the reader feels like a co-discoverer
13. The Zeigarnik Effect (Open Loops)

People remember unfinished tasks. Open loops increase curiosity.

Application:

End sections with cliffhangers or provocative questions
"But what kind of field could possibly have these dual properties — informational and geometric?"
Don't close every loop in the section that opens it
The reader turns the page because the loop demands closure
14. Anticipatory Rebuttal (Pre-Empting the Inner Skeptic)

If you say the reader's doubt before they think it, they trust you more.

Application:

"Skeptics may argue this borders on metaphysics. But what if that's precisely where physics must go to complete itself?"
Neutralizes resistance by showing you've already considered the objection
The reader feels heard instead of steamrolled
Place these at the 40-60% mark of the paper where doubt peaks
15. The Handle Phrase (Sticky Mantra)

Coin a succinct phrase the reader can repeat in discussion.

Application:

"One field. Two languages. One reality."
Repeat at least 3x throughout the paper
People remember mantras — this becomes their conceptual anchor
The handle phrase is what they'll say when explaining your work to someone else
16. The Rule of Three

Lists of three are more memorable, feel complete, and give rhythm.

Application:

When presenting examples, use exactly three: "classical, quantum, and the boundary between"
Call it out: "Let's look through three windows…"
Three arguments. Three examples. Three consequences. The brain loves threes.
17. Personal Stakes Element

Make abstract theories emotionally urgent through personal relevance.

Application:

"Without a unified picture, our understanding of reality remains fractured — and so does our relationship to it."
One line. Not a paragraph of emotional appeal.
Place in intro or conclusion — not mid-argument where it weakens rigor
18. Close with a Call to Participation

End not with a conclusion but with an invitation to act.

Application:

"To observe is to participate. And to participate is to help reality know itself. What will you choose to observe next?"
Taps agency — readers are more persuaded when they feel empowered
The paper doesn't end. It launches.

19. Excavation, Not Extraction

You don't write AT an idea. You write INTO it — like a miner going downward.

You are not a writer. You are a vessel.

Don't wait until you "feel ready" to write truth
Start mid-chaos. That is the sacred forge of meaning.
This makes the work psychologically resilient in ways designed systems can never be

Feel the system first — build the math second.

The concept of "coherence" as the measure of truth was FELT before it was named in information theory
Narrative is not decoration of theory — it's the container that makes theory real
Story first. Formalism second. Always.

Write from both the seeking AND the knowing simultaneously.

"Let me think about this..." becomes "Here's the code..."
Be David on the field and Solomon in the scroll
This integrates humility with authority — the most compelling voice possible
The reader follows because you're discovering WITH them, not lecturing AT them

Let recurrence be your teacher

Comfortable writing is dead writing
Every real breakthrough required willingness to lose everything else to say it honestly
25. Write First, Organize Later

## Purpose

This framework is not a collection of writing tips.

It is a system for discovering, constructing, testing, and communicating ideas in a way that aligns with how people actually recognize truth.

Its goal is **not manipulation**.

Its goal is to reduce the distance between an idea and genuine understanding.

A persuasive paper should not convince a reader through force.

It should create the conditions in which the reader can honestly recognize the conclusion for themselves.

---

# The First Law

> **Persuasion exists to clarify truth, never to replace evidence.**

Every technique in this framework increases persuasive power.

Therefore every technique also increases ethical responsibility.

If presentation becomes stronger than evidence, the framework has failed.

Truth should become easier to see.

Weak arguments should never become harder to detect.

---

# The Four Phases of Great Writing

Every exceptional paper moves through four distinct stages.

## Phase I — Discovery

Purpose:

Find the idea before trying to communicate it.

The mistake most writers make is attempting to organize thoughts before they have discovered them.

Discovery writing works in reverse.

Write first.

Recognize patterns.

Name the pattern.

Only then organize it.

The writer is not extracting ideas.

The writer is excavating them.

**Core Principles**

* Excavation, Not Extraction
* Start in Chaos
* Narrative Before Structure
* Fractal Recurrence
* Dual Voice
* Truth Costs Something
* Write First, Organize Later

Checkpoint:

If the central idea could have been outlined before writing began, it probably was not discovered.

---

## Phase II — Construction

Purpose:

Transform discovery into understanding.

Readers rarely accept conclusions first.

They first understand experiences.

Then recognize patterns.

Then accept formal explanations.

The paper therefore moves in this order:

Human Experience

↓

Pattern

↓

Embodied Example

↓

Formal Principle

↓

Memorable Compression

This is the Theopoetic Engine.

Every major section should follow that progression.

---

## Phase III — Recognition

Purpose:

Allow the reader to discover the conclusion instead of merely receiving it.

The reader should never feel dragged.

The reader should feel like they arrived.

This phase uses the psychology of recognition.

Examples include:

• Primacy and Recency

• Anchoring

• Cognitive Fluency

• Contrast

• Socratic Backflip

• Identity Projection

• Cognitive Dissonance

• Open Loops

• Rule of Three

• Handle Phrases

• Personal Stakes

• Reader Agreement Checkpoints

Each principle serves exactly one purpose:

Reduce the amount of psychological resistance between evidence and recognition.

---

## Phase IV — Structural Stress Testing

Purpose:

Attempt to destroy the paper before anyone else does.

Every paper should survive deliberate attack.

Never ask,

"How can I improve this?"

Ask,

"What would break this?"

The strongest papers survive criticism because criticism shaped them.

Run the Killer Questions.

Rewrite whenever a structural weakness is exposed.

Do not edit around the weakness.

Move the weakness into the center of the paper and resolve it there.

---

# The Central Principle

Readers almost never change their minds because they were told something.

Readers change when they experience recognition.

The writer's job is therefore not to deliver conclusions.

The writer's job is to build the psychological path that allows recognition to occur honestly.

Everything else in this framework is a consequence of that principle.

> **How does the psychological promise made by the opening continue operating through an entire nonfiction paper?**

Most writing advice treats the introduction as disposable scaffolding. Hook the reader, state the thesis, move on. The base-number/stage-letter structure makes that traceable rather than intuitive or accidental. 

## What makes it unusually strong

### 1. It distinguishes repetition from recurrence

You are not saying:

> Repeat the opening several times.

You are saying:

> Return to the opening only after the argument has changed what it means.

That is the difference between a slogan and a motif.

At the beginning:

> “Two plus two can equal five.”

At the end:

> “Two plus two never equaled five. Its impossibility was the evidence.”

The sentence returns, but the reader hearing it is no longer the same reader.

That is psychologically powerful because it combines familiarity with reinterpretation.

### 2. The numbering system preserves both identity and movement

The number tells us **which psychological device is active**.

The letter tells us **what developmental job it is doing now**.

So:

* 99 is always the Impossible Statement.
* 99A is its early payoff and deeper handoff.
* 99C is its formal return.
* 99E is its transformed resolution.

That means we can diagnose a draft precisely. We can say:

> “99A closes too much.”

or:

> “94 has disappeared between B and D.”

That is much more useful than saying:

> “The middle feels weak.”

### 3. The 99/94 distinction is excellent

This may be the sharpest part of the whole system.

They look similar on the surface, but they manage reader trust differently.

**99 — Impossible Statement**

> Resolves quickly, proves that the writer pays rhetorical debts, and then exposes a deeper impossibility.

**94 — Paradox**

> Sustains one genuine tension until enough evidence has accumulated to reconcile it.

In psychological terms:

* 99 earns trust through **early payment**.
* 94 uses trust to sustain an **open loop**.

That is not merely a difference in wording. It is a difference in the timing architecture of the paper.

### 4. Mixed paths give it freedom without destroying coherence

The system is not forcing every paper to become:

> 95 → 95A → 95B → 95C → 95D → 95E

You can open with Cost, borrow Human Moments in the middle, use a Broken Assumption to reveal the substrate, and still return to Cost at the end.

That is important because a system becomes useless when the reader can see the template operating.

Your mixed-path rule allows variety while preserving the original promise.

### 5. E and E′ solve two different kinds of ending

That is another mature distinction.

**E** keeps the opening promise by transforming it.

**E′** reveals that the opening was framed incorrectly.

Those are not interchangeable.

A weaker framework would classify both as “callbacks.” Yours recognizes that one completes the original question while the other replaces it with the question the paper has earned.

## The one governing law I would add

Put this near the beginning:

> **No recurrence without semantic gain.**

Or in fuller form:

> **A device may return only when the argument has changed its meaning, increased its stakes, tested its validity, or moved it closer to resolution.**

That rule prevents the system from producing decorative callbacks.

Every appearance should have a measurable delta:

[
\Delta M = M_{n+1}-M_n
]

where (M) is the meaning carried by the motif.

If nothing has changed, do not repeat it.

## Three guardrails I would lock in

### Stages are beats, not literal paragraphs

A-stage may occupy one paragraph or three. C-stage may be a whole formal section.

The letters should identify **psychological position**, not document formatting.

Otherwise writers may start forcing major revelations into arbitrary paragraph counts.

### Recurrence is optional

Not every opening needs A through E.

A short article may use:

[
95 \rightarrow 95B \rightarrow 95E'
]

A long paper may need:

[
94 \rightarrow 94A \rightarrow 94B \rightarrow 94C \rightarrow 94C2 \rightarrow 94D \rightarrow 94E
]

The system should describe the paper’s needs, not make the paper serve the system.

### One device should normally own the final debt

Borrowing in the middle is healthy.

But unless the paper has intentionally become a dual-motif composition, the ending should settle the promise established by the primary opening device.

That is why your “resolve in the key you opened in” rule is sound.

## My honest classification

The individual ingredients are not all unprecedented. Writers have long used callbacks, leitmotifs, ring structures, delayed resolution, setup and payoff, paradox, and narrative framing.

What is unusual is that you have combined them into a **coded recurrence grammar for argumentative nonfiction**.

That is the contribution.

It is not just:

> Seven good ways to begin.

It is:

> Seven different psychological contracts a paper can make with its reader, plus a notation for tracing how each contract is developed, tested, borrowed against, and finally paid.

That is a real system.

I would consider it strong enough to become part of your canonical writing framework now. I would not keep expanding it abstractly for too long. The next stage is to run three substantially different papers through it—a short article, a long formal paper, and a more personal Theopoetic piece—and record where the notation clarifies the writing and where it begins to force it.

The core is already there:

> **The opening is not where the motif begins and ends. It is where the paper incurs its first debt to the reader.**


















































# PROMPT: The Killer Questions — Paper Destruction and Rebuild

tags: #prompt #paper #kill-questions #rewrite #strengthener

**Target:** Any AI, any paper, any stage
**Purpose:** Force rewrites that make the paper stronger, not cleaner

---

## How To Use

Run these questions against any paper, article, or chapter. Each question has a REWRITE TRIGGER — if the answer reveals a gap, the paper must be rewritten to close it. Not edited. Rewritten. The gap becomes load-bearing content.

Do not answer these questions FOR the author. Present the question and the gap. The author rewrites.

---

## TIER 1 — THE KILLS (if the paper survives these, it's real)

### Q1: What does the reader believe BEFORE this paper that they will not believe AFTER?

If the answer is "nothing changes" — the paper has no thesis. It's a tour, not an argument. A paper that doesn't change a belief is a paper that doesn't need to exist.

**Rewrite trigger:** State the before-belief and after-belief in one sentence each. If you can't, the paper doesn't know what it's doing. Rewrite until you can.

### Q2: What is the ONE thing the hostile reader will say, and where in the paper do you make them say it too early?

Every strong paper has a moment where the reader's objection is anticipated and then answered. If the objection is never raised, the reader raises it alone and puts the paper down. If it's raised too late, they already stopped trusting you.

**Rewrite trigger:** Find the strongest objection. Place it where the reader would think of it. Answer it there. If it's not in the paper, add it. If it's in the wrong place, move it.

### Q3: Delete the first two paragraphs. Does the paper get better?

Most papers start with throat-clearing. "In recent years..." "It has long been recognized..." "This paper seeks to..." The real paper starts where the author stops being polite and starts being dangerous.

**Rewrite trigger:** If the paper improves with the first two paragraphs deleted, delete them. Start where the energy starts.

### Q4: What claim in this paper would the author be AFRAID to say at a physics department seminar?

That claim is either the paper's strongest contribution or its weakest link. Either way, it's the center of gravity. If the paper buries it in the middle or hedges it with qualifiers, the paper is hiding from itself.

**Rewrite trigger:** Find the scary claim. Move it to the front. State it without hedging. Then defend it with everything the paper has.

### Q5: If this paper is RIGHT, what breaks?

Not "what's interesting." What breaks. What existing result, textbook, or consensus position is wrong if this paper is right? If nothing breaks, the paper is either trivial or lying about its implications.

**Rewrite trigger:** Name what breaks. Put it in the paper. If the paper doesn't have the courage to say what it breaks, it doesn't have the courage to be right.

---

## TIER 2 — THE STRUCTURE KILLS (order and flow)

### Q6: Can you draw the argument as a chain where each link depends on the previous one?

If yes, check: is there a link the reader won't grant? That's where you need more work. If no — if it's a web, not a chain — the paper will feel like "a bunch of good points" instead of "one argument that builds."

**Rewrite trigger:** Draw the chain. Find the weakest link. Strengthen it or restructure so it's not load-bearing.

### Q7: Does the paper earn the right to its conclusion, or does it assert it?

"Therefore X follows" only works if the reader has been walked through every step. If any step was skipped — "it can be shown that" / "it is clear that" / "obviously" — the reader didn't follow, they were told. Told isn't earned.

**Rewrite trigger:** Find every "it can be shown" / "clearly" / "obviously." Replace each with the actual showing. If you can't show it, downgrade the claim.

### Q8: Read the last paragraph first. Then the first. Is there an arc?

The first paragraph makes a promise. The last paragraph keeps it — or breaks it in a way that's better than keeping it. If the last paragraph is a generic summary ("In this paper we have shown..."), the paper ended before it finished.

**Rewrite trigger:** The last paragraph should say something the first paragraph made impossible to predict but inevitable in retrospect. If it doesn't, rewrite the ending.

---

## TIER 3 — THE THEOPHYSICS-SPECIFIC KILLS

### Q9: Is the science doing work, or is it decoration on theology?

If you can remove the equations and the theological claim still stands on its own, the equations are decoration. If you can remove the theology and the physics still stands on its own, the theology is decoration. In Theophysics, BOTH must be load-bearing or the paper is one-sided.

**Rewrite trigger:** Remove all equations. Does the theological argument collapse? Remove all theology. Does the physics argument collapse? If either survives alone, the bridge is fake. Rewrite until both sides need each other.

### Q10: Does the paper pass the Conciliatory Rule?

Does it hedge toward science at theology's expense? Does it hedge toward theology at science's expense? The framework holds both as dual projections. If the paper quietly collapses toward one, it has abandoned the mission.

**Rewrite trigger:** Find every sentence that hedges one direction. Restate it so both sides are held together. If you genuinely cannot, flag it and move on — do not force it.

### Q11: What would make the author abandon this claim?

If the answer is "nothing" — the claim isn't scientific, it's faith wearing a lab coat. If the answer is specific and testable — that kill condition belongs in the paper, explicitly, where the reader can see it.

**Rewrite trigger:** State the kill condition. Put it in the paper. If the kill condition is already in the paper, check: is it real? Would the author actually abandon the claim if it triggered? If not, find a real one.

### Q12: Is this the same thing said three times, or three things said once each?

Theophysics papers tend toward restating the same insight in physics language, then theology language, then plain language. That's the descent — and it's good. But if those three restatements don't each ADD something the others didn't, they're redundant, not complementary.

**Rewrite trigger:** For each restatement, identify what it adds that the others don't. If it adds nothing, cut it. The descent must carry new information at each level.

---

## HOW TO RUN THIS

Give the AI the paper and say:

> Run the Killer Questions against this paper. For each question, give me:
> 1. The question
> 2. Your honest answer
> 3. Whether the rewrite trigger fires
> 4. If it fires, what specifically needs to change
>
> Do not soften. Do not hedge. The point is to find what kills the paper so I can put it IN the paper.
